# 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are now served at a scale where inference, not training, dominates the hardware, energy and cost budgets of AI infrastructure. Each request to a decoder-only LLM is processed in two phases with opposite resource profiles. The *prefill* phase processes the whole prompt in parallel, has high arithmetic intensity and is compute-bound. The *decode* phase generates output tokens one at a time, re-reads the model weights and the growing key–value (KV) cache at every step, and is bound by memory bandwidth and capacity [@yuan2024; @patel2024split]. Serving systems must meet two latency service-level objectives (SLOs) that map onto these phases: the time to first token (TTFT) and the time per output token (TPOT) [@zhong2024; @agrawal2024].

Two developments of 2024–2026 change how this problem should be approached. First, prefill–decode (P/D) disaggregation has moved from research prototypes to production. DistServe [@zhong2024], Splitwise [@patel2024split] and Mooncake [@qin2025] run the two phases on separate instances and move the KV cache between them, which removes prefill–decode interference and lets each phase be sized for its own SLO. Second, the accelerator landscape has become both richer and more heterogeneous. Within two years, operators gained access to NVIDIA Hopper (H100, H200) and Blackwell (B200) GPUs, AMD CDNA3 and CDNA4 accelerators (MI300X, MI325X, MI355X) and Intel Gaudi 3 [@nv_h200; @nv_b200; @amd_mi300x; @amd_mi325x; @amd_mi355x; @intel_gaudi3]. These parts differ by more than 5× in peak low-precision throughput, by 3.6× in HBM capacity and by 2.4× in memory bandwidth. They also differ in the numeric formats they execute natively: only the newest generation runs 4-bit floating point (NVFP4 or MXFP4 [@rouhani2023]) at twice its FP8 rate. Production fleets therefore mix several generations, and new accelerators usually arrive in small quantities next to a large installed base.

These developments raise a deployment question that neither line of prior work answers. Hardware-centric studies, including the recent survey by Kim et al. in this journal [@kim2026] and the hardware perspective of Li et al. [@li2024hw], characterize how optimization techniques map onto GPUs, FPGAs, ASICs and processing-in-memory devices. They do not decide, for a given model, workload and SLO, *which* accelerator, *which* numeric precision and *how much* parallelism each serving phase should receive. Systems work on heterogeneous serving, such as Splitwise [@patel2024split], HexGen and HexGen-2 [@jiang2024; @jiang2025], Helix [@mei2025] and Mélange [@griggs2024], does make allocation decisions. However, it was evaluated on A100/H100-era GPUs, treats numeric precision as fixed, optimizes throughput or rental cost rather than energy, and does not consider mixture-of-experts (MoE) models with multi-head latent attention (MLA) [@dsv2; @dsv3] or reasoning workloads whose outputs are thousands of tokens long [@dsr1; @snell2024].

This paper studies that question for the 2024–2026 hardware generation. It asks four research questions:

- **RQ1.** Can a light-weight analytical model, calibrated once on public measurements, predict LLM serving performance across vendors, precisions and model families accurately enough to drive deployment decisions?
- **RQ2.** In a disaggregated deployment, how much energy do the three degrees of freedom (accelerator type, numeric precision and parallelism, each chosen per phase) save relative to colocated serving and to homogeneous disaggregation?
- **RQ3.** When does assigning *different* accelerators to the two phases pay off, and when does a single accelerator type suffice?
- **RQ4.** How do these answers change for fleets that already own older accelerators, and for reasoning models whose cost is dominated by decode?

To answer them we develop **PhaseFit**, a planner that jointly selects, for each phase of a disaggregated deployment, the accelerator type, the numeric precision (BF16, FP8 or FP4), the parallelism degree and the number of instances that minimize energy per request while meeting p99 TTFT and TPOT targets. The contributions are as follows.

1. **A phase-aware, cross-vendor performance and power model** (Section 4.2). The model computes the duration of every forward iteration from operator-level FLOPs and bytes. It captures GQA and MLA attention, MoE expert routing, tensor and expert parallelism, block-scaled FP8/FP4 formats and a utilization-dependent power draw. Five parameters are fitted once on 22 published TensorRT-LLM measurements. The model is then validated, without refitting, on 32 held-out measurements covering an unseen model (Qwen3-235B-A22B), an unseen vendor and software stack (vLLM on 8× MI300X) and MLPerf Inference v5.1 reasoning results.
2. **A decomposition result and a planner** (Sections 4.3–4.5). We show that under disaggregation the p99 TTFT depends only on the prefill pool and the p99 TPOT only on the decode pool plus a KV-transfer term. The planning problem therefore separates into two per-phase problems coupled only through KV transfer and the pairing policy. PhaseFit solves each per-phase problem by analytical screening followed by simulation-guided minimal sizing. It extends naturally to fleet-constrained (*brownfield*) settings in which scarce new accelerators share a phase with older ones.
3. **A trace-driven evaluation on 2024–2026 hardware** (Sections 5–6). We drive a discrete-event simulator with the public Azure LLM inference traces [@azure2023; @patel2024split] and with a reasoning workload whose length statistics match the MLPerf v5.1 DeepSeek-R1 benchmark [@mlc_r1]. We evaluate three model families (dense GQA, MoE with GQA and MoE with MLA), seven accelerators and three precisions, and we compare PhaseFit against colocated serving, homogeneous disaggregation and Splitwise-style hardware-only heterogeneity.
4. **Two case studies and design guidelines** (Sections 6.5–7). The first adds scarce Blackwell GPUs to a Hopper fleet and finds where they save the most energy. The second deploys a reasoning model under MLPerf SLOs. We distil the results into guidelines for system architects and identify the conditions under which phase-level heterogeneity helps and those under which it does not.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 gives background on the two inference phases and on the accelerators studied. Section 3 reviews related work. Section 4 presents the PhaseFit model and planner. Section 5 describes the experimental methodology, Section 6 reports the results and Section 7 discusses implications and threats to validity. Section 8 concludes.

# 2. Background and motivation

## 2.1. The two phases of decoder-only inference

Consider a decoder-only transformer with $L$ layers, hidden size $d$, $n_h$ query heads of dimension $d_h$, $n_{kv}$ key–value heads and $N_{act}$ parameters active per token ($N_{act}=N$ for dense models; for MoE models only the $k$ routed experts selected out of $E$ count). For a prompt of $s$ tokens, the prefill phase performs approximately

$$F_{pre}(s) = 2N_{act}\,s + 2L\,n_h d_h\,s^2 \qquad (1)$$

floating-point operations. The first term covers the linear layers and the second the causal attention over $s^2/2$ query–key pairs. Producing one output token at context length $c$ during decode costs

$$F_{dec}(c) = 2N_{act} + 4L\,n_h d_h\,c . \qquad (2)$$

Each decode step must also read all resident weights and the KV cache of every sequence in the batch. The KV cache stores, per token,

$$K = L\cdot 2\,n_{kv}d_h\cdot b_{kv}\ \ \text{(GQA)},\qquad K = L\,(d_c + d_r)\,b_{kv}\ \ \text{(MLA)} \qquad (3)$$

bytes, where $b_{kv}$ is the element size and, for MLA, $d_c$ and $d_r$ are the dimensions of the compressed latent and of the decoupled rotary key [@dsv2]. For a decode batch of $B$ sequences with mean context $\bar c$ and weights stored with $b_w$ bytes per parameter, the arithmetic intensity of one step is

$$I(B) = \frac{B\,F_{dec}(\bar c)}{N\,b_w + B\,\bar c\,K}\ \ \text{FLOP/byte}. \qquad (4)$$

The step is memory-bound while $I(B)$ is below the ridge point $P/BW$ of the accelerator (peak FLOP/s over memory bandwidth) [@williams2009]. For Llama-3.1-70B in FP8 on an H200 the ridge point is about 412 FLOP/byte, whereas $I(1)\approx 2$. Decode becomes compute-bound only at batch sizes in the hundreds, and the KV term in the denominator of Eq. (4) grows with both $B$ and $\bar c$. Prefill, in contrast, reaches the ridge point with a few hundred prompt tokens. This asymmetry is the root of the phase-specific hardware preferences studied in this paper.

## 2.2. The 2024–2026 accelerator generation and numeric formats

Table 1 lists the accelerators considered. Three trends matter for inference. First, dense low-precision throughput grew faster than memory bandwidth. From H100 to B200 the FP8 peak grows 2.3×, the memory bandwidth 2.4× and the capacity 2.25×. B200 and MI355X also add native FP4, which doubles the peak again and halves the weight footprint. Second, memory capacity diverged between vendors: an MI355X carries 288 GB of HBM3E against 180 GB for a B200, which directly bounds the KV cache, and therefore the decode batch, that one device can hold. Third, block-scaled formats (MXFP8, MXFP4 and NVFP4 [@rouhani2023; @micikevicius2022]) made sub-8-bit inference accurate enough for industry benchmarks. MLPerf Inference v5.0 and v5.1 closed-division submissions on Blackwell used FP4 for Llama 2 70B, Llama 3.1 405B and DeepSeek-R1 while meeting the 99% accuracy targets [@nv_mlperf50; @nv_mlperf51].

## 2.3. Motivation: per-phase efficiency differs, but less than expected

Fig. 1 shows the energy each accelerator–precision pair needs per 1,000 prompt tokens in prefill and per output token in decode, as predicted by the model of Section 4 for Llama-3.1-70B. Three observations motivate this work. (i) Within one accelerator, precision matters as much as hardware generation. Moving from BF16 to FP8 halves the energy of both phases on every device except Gaudi 3, whose FP8 and BF16 matrix peaks are equal, and FP4 saves about a further 20% on B200 and MI355X. (ii) Across accelerators, the ranking is largely shared by both phases. The newest FP4-capable parts lead in both, so heterogeneity is not *automatically* beneficial, contrary to what earlier studies on A100/H100 pairs suggested [@patel2024split]. (iii) The gaps between devices differ by phase. Gaudi 3 is the least efficient FP8 device in decode, yet it is the second most efficient BF16 device in prefill. The H200's extra bandwidth and capacity lower its decode energy relative to the H100 but leave prefill unchanged. MI325X is less efficient than MI300X in both phases because its higher board power (1,000 W against 750 W) buys memory, not compute. Whether such second-order differences, together with SLOs, granularity and fleet constraints, justify heterogeneous deployments is the question the rest of the paper answers quantitatively.

# 3. Related work

**Serving systems and scheduling.** Orca introduced iteration-level (continuous) batching [@yu2022], and vLLM introduced paged KV-cache management [@kwon2023], later complemented by vAttention, which keeps the cache virtually contiguous [@prabhu2025]. Sarathi-Serve splits prompts into chunks and piggybacks decodes on them to avoid generation stalls [@agrawal2024]. SGLang reuses KV caches across requests through a radix tree [@zheng2024], and Llumnix reschedules requests across instances at run time [@sun2024]. Attention kernels such as FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 [@dao2024; @shah2024] set the compute efficiency of both phases. PhaseFit uses chunked-prefill colocated serving as its strongest non-disaggregated baseline.

**Prefill–decode disaggregation.** Splitwise [@patel2024split] showed on Azure production traces that the token phase does not need the latest GPU, and it designed homogeneous and heterogeneous (H100 + A100) clusters that deliver up to 1.4× higher throughput at 20% lower cost. DistServe [@zhong2024] co-optimizes resource allocation and parallelism per phase for goodput and serves up to 7.4× more requests than colocated systems. Mooncake [@qin2025] builds a KV-cache-centric disaggregated architecture for the Kimi service and reports 115% and 107% more requests on A800 and H800 clusters. These systems either use one GPU type or a fixed older pair, and none selects numeric precision per phase.

**Heterogeneous serving.** HexGen [@jiang2024] and HexGen-2 [@jiang2025] schedule inference over heterogeneous GPUs, the latter with disaggregation, using graph partitioning and max-flow. Helix [@mei2025] formulates serving over heterogeneous GPUs and networks as a max-flow problem solved with mixed-integer programming. Mélange [@griggs2024] selects the most cost-efficient mix of GPU types from request size, rate and SLO, and reports up to 77% lower deployment cost than a single GPU type. These works target rental cost or throughput on A100/H100-era hardware with fixed precision. PhaseFit differs in three respects: it treats precision as a per-phase decision variable, it optimizes energy with a utilization-dependent power model, and it covers 2025-generation FP4 accelerators, MoE/MLA models and inventory-constrained fleets.

**Quantization and numeric formats.** Weight-only and weight–activation quantization (AWQ [@lin2024awq], SmoothQuant [@xiao2023], QServe [@lin2025qserve]), KV-cache quantization (KIVI [@liu2024kivi]) and hardware formats (FP8 [@micikevicius2022], MX [@rouhani2023]) reduce the bytes moved per token. Architectural changes such as GQA [@ainslie2023] and MLA [@dsv2] shrink the KV cache. PhaseFit does not propose a new format. It decides where each format pays off once the accuracy of that format has been established, for example by MLPerf closed-division results [@nv_mlperf51].

**Performance and energy modelling.** Roofline analysis [@williams2009; @yuan2024] explains phase behaviour but does not predict latency under queueing. Vidur [@agrawal2024vidur] predicts request-level latency with under 9% error from per-operator profiles collected on the target GPU and searches homogeneous configurations. LLMCompass [@zhang2024compass] evaluates hypothetical hardware designs. Neither covers multi-vendor deployments without profiling access. On the energy side, Samsi et al. [@samsi2023] measured inference energy, POLCA [@patel2024power] exploited power headroom to deploy 30% more servers, and DynamoLLM [@stojkovic2025] reconfigures instances, parallelism and GPU frequency, saving 53% energy at the service level. PhaseFit is complementary: it decides the hardware, precision and pool composition that such run-time controllers then operate on.

**Hardware-centric surveys.** Kim et al. [@kim2026] organize LLM inference acceleration by hardware platform (GPU, FPGA, ASIC, PIM) and by operator- versus system-level optimization, and Li et al. [@li2024hw] compare platforms on throughput and tokens per joule. Processing-in-memory designs such as AttAcc [@park2024attacc] and NeuPIMs [@heo2024] exploit the memory-bound nature of decode attention. The present work turns such characterizations into a deployment decision procedure for the accelerators that operators can buy today. Table 2 summarizes the differences.
